Clinical early warning must balance detection against a manageable alert load, yet discrimination metrics do not specify how often a deployed rule will alarm. We formulate early warning as a stateful alarm policy with a threshold, refractory window and escalation tier, and calibrate it against a patient-average daily alarm budget. The artificial-intelligence contribution is a reusable layer that converts a fixed risk score into an auditable policy: patient-level losses are combined with a finite-grid upper-confidence bound, a Learn-then-Test correction, a conformal risk control selector, a validation-ranked utility selector and a randomised mixture. The engineering application is a causal sepsis pipeline on 40{,}336 PhysioNet 2019 records from two hospitals with three scorers over three subject-disjoint seeds, an independent 2012 mortality stress test, official 2019 utility, decision-curve net benefit and a queue simulation. On site A, mean sensitivity rises from 0.185 at 0.25 alarms per patient-day to 0.774 at 2 per day. Applying the source threshold to site B lowers sensitivity at 1 per day from 0.585 to 0.315 despite a lower realised rate; conformal risk control raises B sensitivity to 0.482 at a higher rate, while the official utility reverses the ranking because it penalises false alarms; a utility-ranked variant avoids over-alarming at loose budgets, raising utility from 0.56 to 0.93 at 2 per day. The 2012 test shows that the budget travels while the operating point does not. These results support budget calibration as an engineering instrument, not a clinical claim.
