Clinical early warning must balance detection against a manageable alert load, yet threshold-independent discrimination metrics do not specify how often a deployed rule will alarm. We formulate early warning as a stateful alarm policy with a threshold, refractory window and escalation tier, and calibrate it against a budget on the patient-average number of alarms per day. The procedure aggregates within-patient alarm sequences into patient-level losses and applies a finite-grid upper-confidence bound under independent patient sampling, with scorer, policy family and support bound fixed before calibration. A no-alarm fallback is returned when no threshold is certified. We evaluate the procedure on the 40{,}336 PhysioNet 2019 records from two hospital systems, using a causal prefix logistic model and a masked gated recurrent unit (GRU) over three subject-disjoint seeds. Mean sensitivity rises from 0.185 at a budget of 0.25 alarms per patient-day to 0.774 at 2 per day. Applying the source threshold to the second hospital without target calibration lowers sensitivity at 1 per day from 0.585 to 0.315, even though the realised rate there is lower. An oracle-style target-site recalibration using target labels raises sensitivity from 0.294 to 0.497 at 1 per day but also raises the realised rate from 0.270 to 0.616; adaptation changes the operating point rather than eliminating the shift. A timestep effective-sample-size comparator is less conservative at tight budgets and increases per-patient budget violations. The results support patient-blocked budget calibration as an auditable engineering instrument, but they do not establish clinical effectiveness, alert-fatigue reduction or deployment readiness.
