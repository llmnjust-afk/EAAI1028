Early warning must balance detection against alert load, yet discrimination does not specify deployed alarm frequency. We formulate early warning as a stateful alarm policy with a threshold, refractory window and escalation tier, and calibrate it against a patient-average daily alarm budget. The contribution is a reusable layer converting a fixed risk score into an auditable policy: patient-level losses are combined with a finite-grid upper-confidence bound, a Learn-then-Test correction, a conformal risk control selector, a validation-ranked utility selector and a randomised mixture. The engineering application is a causal sepsis pipeline on 40{,}336 PhysioNet 2019 records from two hospitals, three scorers and three subject-disjoint seeds, a separate-endpoint 2012 mortality stress test, official utility, decision curves and a queue simulation. On site A, mean sensitivity rises from 0.185 at 0.25 alarms per patient-day to 0.774 at 2 per day. Applying the source threshold to site B lowers sensitivity at 1 per day from 0.585 to 0.315 despite a lower realised rate; conformal risk control raises B sensitivity to 0.482 at a higher rate, while official utility reverses the ranking because it penalises false alarms. On the primary seed, a utility-ranked variant avoids over-alarming at 2 per day, raising internal utility from 0.562 to 0.932 and external from $-0.225$ to 0.278; its internal utility is 0.61--0.93 across seeds. The 2012 test shows that the requested rate is a portable reporting unit, but neither the certificate nor the operating point transports. These results support budget calibration as an engineering instrument, not a clinical claim.
