\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central empirical finding is that a patient-blocked alarm budget is portable as a \emph{constraint} but not as a \emph{threshold}. Within site A, the calibrated policy moves smoothly from 0.19 sensitivity at 0.25 alarms per patient-day to 0.77 at 2 per day, and the realised rates remain below the requested budgets. When the same A-calibrated threshold is applied to site B, the realised rate falls to roughly half the A rate and sensitivity at 1 per day falls from 0.585 to 0.315. The budget itself is still respected in the sense that B's realised mean is below the requested budget, but the clinical yield is much lower. This distinction matters for engineering practice: a site that imports only a threshold has not imported the operating point that the source site validated.

The comparison with the timestep shortcut clarifies what patient blocking buys, but not in the way a single-split table suggests. In ten repeated splits the shortcut stayed below the mean budget on both sites and often selected the same threshold as the plug-in rule, because its effective sample size is large. The data therefore do not show that timestep calibration fails empirically on this cohort. What they show is that the shortcut has no patient-level guarantee and that its patient-violation fraction is higher at 1 per day ($0.296$ versus $0.210$). When the estimand is a patient-average rate, the effective sample size is the number of independent patients; a method that substitutes correlated hours can be less conservative without acquiring the guarantee it appears to have. The defensible conclusion is about the estimand and the audit trail, not a universal empirical ranking.

For engineering deployment, the results support five concrete practices. First, the minimum observed stay used in the support bound should be declared before looking at calibration or test outcomes, because it changes both eligibility and $L_{\max}$; the 24-hour restriction here removes about 22\% of A records and 26\% of B records. Second, the no-alarm fallback should remain an explicit, auditable state rather than being replaced by the largest finite threshold, since the latter can still alarm and has no certificate. Third, a target site that has local labels should recalibrate on its own patient blocks before adopting a source threshold, because the external rate and sensitivity can move in opposite directions; our oracle split shows that this recovers part of the sensitivity but not without a higher alarm load. A label-free alternative can match the requested rate on unlabelled target traces, a one-shot empirical quantile rule related to adaptive conformal inference \citep{gibbs2021adaptive}, but it controls the alarm rate rather than the risk and therefore cannot replace a labelled certificate. Fourth, the threshold grid and the realised-rate report should be published together, since a grid floor can make high budgets look identical and hide the fact that the constraint is no longer binding. Fifth, a budget is a ceiling rather than a target: at loose budgets the selector should be allowed to stop before the budget is exhausted, because the marginal alarms are predominantly false and the official utility can fall below a tighter operating point. These practices are operational rather than architectural, and they are compatible with the alert-stewardship literature that treats interruptive alerts as a monitored resource \citep{khan2026qualitative,ray2026alert,jaidev2026sepsis}.

The study has several limitations. It is retrospective and uses the two-site 2019 cohort plus the 2012 cohort as an outcome-shift stress test, so no independent same-task hospital cohort is available; the releases cannot link readmissions, so the independent-subject assumption remains an assumption. The U-CRC stage ranks calibration-feasible thresholds on a validation split, but it is a deployment heuristic and does not add a finite-sample utility guarantee. The label is the challenge's sepsis definition rather than an adjudicated deterioration event, left-censored records are excluded, and the score models are deliberately simple reference learners. The partial NEWS, SIRS and qSOFA baselines omit unavailable components, and the label-free adaptation rule is transductive and controls only the alarm rate. Target recalibration and label-budget results are oracle diagnostics that use target labels, not deployment procedures. No subgroup analysis or clinical outcome evaluation was performed, and ECE on a low-prevalence timestep problem is not evidence of clinical calibration. The coverage study uses ten splits per site, the GRU target diagnostic is single-seed, and no GPU-hour or alert-fatigue outcome was measured.

These limitations do not overturn the methodological result, but they change what should be claimed. The paper does not show that budgeted calibration improves patient outcomes, reduces mortality or is preferred by clinicians. It shows that a stateful policy can be calibrated against a patient-level alarm budget with a finite-sample bound, that the resulting operating curve is measurable on a public cohort, and that the same threshold does not transport unchanged across hospitals. That is a narrower contribution than a clinical-effectiveness claim, but it is a more honest one, and it identifies the target-calibration experiment that a deployment study would need to run next.
