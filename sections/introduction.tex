\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deterioration events in hospitalised patients unfold over hours, and the physiological trajectory that precedes them is recorded long before the event itself is recognised. This temporal structure has motivated a substantial literature on data-driven early warning, ranging from static severity scores to sequential models that forecast deterioration trajectories from electronic health records \citep{xiumin2026machine} and compact, efficiency-aware networks that predict sepsis onset from structured time series \citep{taparugssanagorn2026lightweight}. Reported discrimination is high and improving, with in-hospital mortality and onset prediction now routinely exceeding the performance of classical scoring instruments \citep{yong2024deep}. The implicit promise of this line of work is that a sufficiently accurate model, once installed at the bedside, will shorten time to intervention.

That promise has not been realised at scale, and the reason is operational rather than statistical. A prediction model produces a probability at every observation time for every patient; converting those probabilities into alerts requires choosing a threshold, and the threshold that maximises discrimination on a retrospective cohort typically generates an alert volume that a clinical unit cannot absorb. Recent methodological reviews of adverse-event prediction in intensive care document that reported performance is dominated by discrimination metrics, while alert burden, time-to-alert, and calibration under deployment conditions are reported inconsistently or not at all \citep{wang2026deep}. The same imbalance appears in reviews of deep learning for multivariate clinical time series, where architectural novelty is emphasised far more than the operating characteristics of the resulting monitoring system \citep{hernandezdiaz2026deep,swinckels2024deep}. Consequently, two models with similar area under the receiver operating characteristic curve can differ by several-fold in the number of alarms they emit, and the literature offers no principled way to compare them at a common operational cost.

The dominant technical response has been to improve the backbone that produces the risk score. Because clinical observations are irregularly sampled and heterogeneously missing, a rich family of methods models observation times explicitly, including continuous-time and reliability-aware temporal aggregation \citep{kim2026retamamba,shen2026enhancing}, graph formulations over irregular observations \citep{yalavarthi2024grafiti}, and masked-attention models that exploit informative missingness for deterioration prediction in general wards \citep{mun2026made}. Transfer-oriented variants further align feature spaces across cohorts so that a model trained in one setting can be reused in another \citep{komolafe2025early}, and dynamic temporal graphs incorporate clinical calculators directly into the prediction pathway \citep{yin2024sepsiscalc}. These contributions are complementary to ours and are adopted here as backbones, but they leave the operating point untouched: the mapping from scores to alerts is still a threshold selected after training, and the cost of that choice is still unmeasured.

A separate literature has begun to attach reliability guarantees to clinical predictions. Selective prediction and calibration have been analysed systematically for multimodal clinical classification \citep{lpez2026empirical}, reliability estimation has been extended beyond output-space calibration for time-series classifiers \citep{cenacchi2026beyond}, and conformal methods have been used to equip physiological regression with statistically calibrated uncertainty \citep{strickland2025degrees}. These treatments share an assumption that is violated by early warning systems in operation: they calibrate a decision rule applied independently to exchangeable examples. An alerting system is not a sequence of independent decisions. Once an alarm fires, clinical practice and good engineering alike suppress subsequent alarms for a refractory period, so the decision at time $t$ depends on the decision at $t-1$. This internal state breaks the exchangeability that standard conformal calibration relies on, and the resulting guarantee is silently invalid precisely in the regime where alarms cluster.

This paper closes that gap by making the alarm budget an explicit constraint and calibrating the policy that enforces it. Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate early warning as a stateful alarm policy in which the refractory window and escalation tier are control variables, so that alarm volume becomes a design parameter of the monitoring system rather than an emergent property of a threshold.
\item We show that timestep-level conformal calibration is invalid for such policies and develop a patient-level blocked conformal risk control procedure that restores finite-sample control of the population alarm rate, with an explicit bound on the probability that it exceeds the budget.
\item We propose an evaluation protocol that compares methods at matched alarm budgets, and we instantiate it on open multi-hospital cohorts under cross-hospital transfer, prevalence shift, and observation sparsification.
\end{itemize}
The remainder of the paper reviews related work (\cref{sec:related}), develops the policy formulation and the calibration procedure (\cref{sec:method}), and specifies the datasets, baselines, metrics, and experimental protocol (\cref{sec:setup}).
