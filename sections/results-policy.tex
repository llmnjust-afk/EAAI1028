\subsection{Budgeted Alarm Policies and Cross-Site Transport}

Figure~\ref{fig:budget} and Table~\ref{tab:e2} report the operational frontier for the prefix logistic scorer. On site A, raising the requested budget from 0.25 to 2 alarms per patient-day increases mean sensitivity from $0.185\pm0.061$ to $0.774\pm0.032$, while the realised alarm rate rises from $0.108\pm0.012$ to $1.366\pm0.026$. The realised rate stays below the requested budget at every operating point, which is the expected consequence of a finite-sample upper bound rather than evidence that the policy is slack by design. The tightest budget has the largest seed-to-seed sensitivity variance, because a small change in the calibrated threshold moves several of the 168 eligible A events across the detection boundary. Median lead time among detected events stays between 8.7 and 10 hours, but this conditional median must be read with the corresponding sensitivity: it describes only the events that were detected.

The external audit exposes a qualitatively different operating point. At the 1-per-day budget the A-calibrated policy emits $0.276\pm0.063$ alarms per B patient-day, less than half the A rate, and detects $0.315\pm0.062$ of eligible B events compared with $0.585\pm0.020$ at A. At the tightest budget, B sensitivity falls to $0.081\pm0.026$ while the realised B alarm rate is only $0.040\pm0.014$. The direction of the rate change is not a free lunch: the same threshold is simply less active on the shifted score distribution, and the lost activity is concentrated on true events. This is exactly the failure mode that a threshold-only transport claim would hide, and it is consistent with recent evidence that score distributions and calibration can shift across hospitals \citep{lu2022estimating,wu2026outofdistribution}. A label-free target-site adaptation study is therefore a separate deployment step, not an optional refinement.

The no-recalibration audit establishes that the source threshold does not travel, but it does not quantify how much of the loss is recoverable by adaptation. To estimate that quantity, we split target B into subject-disjoint calibration and test halves (7,412 patients each; on average 354 calibration events and 337 test events over three seeds), recalibrated the same policy family on B calibration labels, and evaluated it on the B test half. This is an oracle diagnostic: target labels are used for threshold selection, so it is not a deployable label-free procedure and must not be compared with the primary external audit as if it were one. Table~\ref{tab:target} reports the result for the logistic scorer. At 1 alarm per patient-day, target calibration raises mean B sensitivity from $0.294\pm0.082$ to $0.497\pm0.022$ and the realised rate from $0.270\pm0.065$ to $0.616\pm0.058$; at 0.25 per day it raises sensitivity from $0.073\pm0.026$ to $0.218\pm0.036$ at a realised rate of $0.159\pm0.009$. The recovery is real but partial, and it is purchased with a higher alarm load and a higher patient-violation fraction because the target-calibrated threshold is lower. The single-seed GRU diagnostic shows the same direction ($0.314$ to $0.493$ at 1 per day). At 2 per day the target-recalibrated selector hits the threshold-grid floor: sensitivity saturates at $0.724\pm0.056$ while the realised rate rises to $1.466\pm0.260$, so the diagnostic identifies a range where adaptation matters rather than a universal gain.



The deliberately invalid timestep comparator is less conservative at the two tightest budgets. At 0.25 per patient-day it raises the mean A alarm rate from $0.108\pm0.012$ to $0.180\pm0.021$ and sensitivity from $0.185\pm0.061$ to $0.283\pm0.033$, with a patient-violation fraction of $0.113\pm0.008$ versus $0.073\pm0.001$. At 0.50 per day the corresponding rate is $0.425\pm0.057$ versus $0.312\pm0.037$, sensitivity is $0.470\pm0.052$ versus $0.397\pm0.067$, and violation is $0.196\pm0.024$ versus $0.151\pm0.014$. At 1 per day the comparator remains more active ($0.744\pm0.158$ versus $0.651\pm0.022$) and less conservative; at 2 per day the finite grid collapses both procedures to the same threshold. The result is therefore a range-specific rather than a universal ranking, but it is the expected qualitative signature of effective-sample-size inflation: the timestep procedure buys sensitivity by shrinking its guard band and paying for it in per-patient budget violations.

The GRU changes the level of the frontier but not its transport behaviour. At 1 alarm per patient-day its three-seed mean A sensitivity is $0.684\pm0.006$ and its B sensitivity is $0.375\pm0.078$, compared with $0.585\pm0.020$ and $0.315\pm0.062$ for the logistic model; the B alarm rate is also higher ($0.328\pm0.164$ versus $0.276\pm0.063$). At 0.25 per day the GRU reaches $0.301\pm0.082$ at A and $0.115\pm0.027$ at B, again above the logistic frontier. The GRU is therefore a secondary architecture check rather than a demonstrated improvement: its seed-level spread is large, its paired site-B sensitivity interval includes zero after Holm adjustment, and its external alarm rate is higher. LightGBM is stronger on site B but, as \cref{sec:models} shows, it still leaves the realised rate below the requested budget and does not remove the need for target calibration. A more expressive scorer can move the external frontier without repairing the site shift, and it can raise external alarm load while doing so. The calibration procedure, not the backbone alone, determines whether a stated alert budget is met.

Two limitations of the grid are visible in the table. First, the 4-per-day row already sits at the threshold grid floor and, in the full sweep, coincides with 8 per day; the grid stops at 0.01; above roughly 2 alarms per patient-day the budget constraint is no longer binding and the frontier is truncated by the candidate set. Second, every certified operating point has a realised rate below its requested budget, so the experiment demonstrates conservative risk control rather than tight budget utilisation. Neither observation undermines the certificate, but both matter for engineering deployment: a hospital that needs a precise alarm load may prefer a finer grid or a utility-based selection among certified thresholds, and a hospital that needs a higher sensitivity must accept the corresponding increase in realised alert volume. The clinical interpretation of these rates also depends on local workflow and alarm-fatigue tolerance, which this retrospective study does not measure \citep{ray2026alert,jaidev2026sepsis}.
